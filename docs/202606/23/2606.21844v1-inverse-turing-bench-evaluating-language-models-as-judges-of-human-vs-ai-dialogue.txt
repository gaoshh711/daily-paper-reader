# Inverse Turing Bench: Evaluating Language Models as Judges of Human vs. AI Dialogue 

William Hager 1 Ishika Rathi 3 Masum Hasan 1 Cameron Jones 21University of Rochester 2Stony Brook University 3Independent Researcher 

whager@u.rochester.edu ishika.m.rathi@gmail.com m.hasan@rochester.edu cameron.jones@stonybrook.edu 

Abstract 

As AI systems integrate into online spaces, differentiating them from humans in conver-sations is increasingly important. We present INVERSE TURING BENCH , a benchmark that evaluates LLMs and other models on their ability to differentiate humans and AI in multi-turn text. The benchmark provides a collection of paired dialogue transcripts, wherein one dialogue is between two humans and the other is between a human and an AI. The task is to correctly identify which dialogue is human-only vs. human-AI. We evaluated a preliminary set of models against this benchmark, and found that GPTZero, Claude Opus-4.6, and GPT-5.5 achieve the highest accuracy: 89.41%, 77.92%, and 75.94% respectively. Our results suggest that statistical approaches to detection have semantic blind spots, but semantic approaches are susceptible to persona-prompting. Our work speaks to the Inverse Turing Test as a probe of LLM theory of mind, and motivates human-AI differenti-ation as a critical capability for AI systems. Our live benchmark can be found at https: //huggingface.co/spaces/roc-hci/ Inverse-Turing-Bench-Leaderboard

1 Introduction 

The internet is shifting from a space of human interaction to a mixed ecosystem of humans and AI agents (Ng and Carley, 2025), making it criti-cal to differentiate between human and AI behav-ior. Most AI detection research focuses on single-turns: whether a text is fully human- or machine-generated (Mitchell et al., 2023a; Wu et al., 2025). But online content is increasingly composed of interactions between multiple human and AI in-terlocutors, posing novel AI detection challenges: multiple speakers, context-dependence, short turns, and colloquial language (Graf et al., 2026). Multi-turn detection poses both a technical and social challenge. In order for people to make in-formed choices about when and how to engage with others, it’s crucial that they know whether their interlocutor is human (Gausen et al., 2026). AI models masquerading as people can cause a variety of harms (Jones and Bergen, 2026a), includ-ing carrying out fraud (Meta, 2026; Bengio et al., 2026), spreading misinformation (Shao et al., 2018; Simchon et al., 2024), and feigning public support through astroturfing (Zerback et al., 2021). Current AI detection can be categorized as: i) classical methods that use statistical metrics such as likelihood to classify text, ii) supervised classifiers fine-tuned on labeled data, and iii) zero- and few-shot LLM-as-judge techniques (Abdali et al., 2024; Doughman et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2023). Each has important limitations. Statistical techniques are sensitive to superficial distributional properties of text, like style or complexity, while zero-shot clas-sifiers struggle with out-of-distribution and adver-sarially prompted outputs (Doughman et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025; Abdali et al., 2024). Rapid growth of model capabilities calls for a live benchmark of detection techniques; how-ever existing detection benchmarks tend to fo-cus on single-turn detection rather than multi-turn dialogue. MGTBench (He et al., 2024) and M4GTBench (Wang et al., 2024) evaluate a wide range of classifiers across several tasks, and TUR-INGBENCH (Uchendu et al., 2021) maintains a live leaderboard on classification tasks. To our knowledge, none support multi-turn dialogue. Beyond the practical value of accurate AI de-tection, researchers have long been interested in the extent to which artificial systems can distin-guish human- and machine-generated outputs. The Inverse Turing test (ITT) is a variation of Turing (1950)’s Imitation Game, which places a machine in the role of a judge that must discriminate be-tween a human and an AI. Watt (1996) proposed the ITT as a measure of a machine’s ‘naive psy-1

As LLM applications become more agentic and inte-grated into society (Anthropic, 2026; Jiang et al., 2026), the ITT takes on new significance. Where LLMs act independently or on behalf of humans, their ability to discriminate between human and AI-generated text becomes crucial to protecting the integrity and safety of human spaces. A live benchmark of detection methods enables us to track progress in machine discriminative ability, even as conversational systems evolve. 

2 Inverse Turing Benchmark 

2.1 Dataset 

Our dataset is drawn from Jones and Bergen (2026b), filtered for dialogues of length 50 or more to ensure sufficient conversational depth. It features 557 pairs of dialogues as part of a 3-party Turing Test, in which a human Interrogator engages in di-alogue with two Witnesses : one human and one AI. In the 3-party test, the interrogator must determine which of these witnesses is human. Figure 2 and Table 2 in the Appendix show the distribution of dialogue length and a sample dialogue pair. The AI witnesses include four different AI sys-tems: Eliza (Weizenbaum, 1966), GPT-4.5 (Ope-nAI, 2025), GPT-4o (OpenAI, 2024), and LLaMA-405B (Meta AI, 2024). The dataset also includes 

Persona variants of GPT-4.5 and LLaMA-405B, which contain an additional system prompt of a college student persona. Appendix Table 3 shows Witness model distribution. 

2.2 Task 

Each item in the task consists of a pair of dialogues, labeled "A" and "B", as input. Each dialogue is a conversation between interrogator ("I") and witness ("W"). The interrogator is always human. Of the two dialogues, exactly one features a human wit-ness, while the other features an AI witness. The classifier must determine which label corresponds to the human–human conversation. Given dialogue transcripts A, B, the judge predicts a binary label: 

who _is _human ∈ { A, B}.

2.3 Live Benchmark 

INVERSE TURING BENCH is publicly available at https://huggingface.co/spaces/roc-hci/ Inverse-Turing-Bench-Leaderboard . It in-cludes a live leaderboard displaying submissions, provides an explanation of the task, and offers a submission portal through which users can submit their own classification labels for evaluation. For submission, users either download the raw dataset containing only the conversation pairs or obtain the provided “starter code," which includes addi-tional framework and infrastructure for evaluation, including the prompt used in this study. 

2.4 Learnable patterns in dataset 

To check for learnable superficial patterns in the dataset, we trained TF-IDF and DistilBERT (Sanh et al., 2020) classifiers using ten-fold cross-validation on labeled data (Table 1). TF-IDF (wit-ness turns only) achieved 47.04% and DistilBERT (full-dialogue) achieved 78.46% accuracy. Distil-BERT’s accuracy indicates that there are clearly syntactic and semantic patterns across dialogues that can be learned. However, this pattern cannot be captured by word frequency alone, as implied by the poor performance of TF-IDF. Existence of such patterns suggests that the benchmark is effective on a zero-shot basis, and may not be as effective if the models are specifically trained with this data. 

3 Preliminary Benchmark Outcomes 

We evaluated a suite of models on the bench-mark task. The models fall into two main cate-gories: token-probability-based judges and LLM-as-a-judge (Table 1). 

3.1 Token Probability Models 

Token-probability-based models calculate how likely a token is to be generated by a language model given a previous string of tokens. High likelihood implies LLM-generated text, and low likelihood implies human-written text (Mitchell et al., 2023b; Wu et al., 2023). We evaluated two token-probability-based models: GPTZero (Adam et al., 2026), a commercial model, and DetectGPT (Mitchell et al., 2023b), an open source model. 

GPTZero is an AI-detection tool that classifies a text string as human-generated, AI-generated, or mixed, and provides probability scores for each label. Using this label as a binary classification task, GPTZero achieved 77.38% classification accuracy on the Witness turns of the individual dialogues. 3.2 LLM-as-a-Judge 

We evaluated a suite of LLMs on this benchmark. For each model, we passed each pair of dialogues as a "zero-shot" classification task. We prompted the LLM to output in JSON format which dialogue is human/human, and to ignore the interrogator turns and focus only on the witness turns (Appendix 4). The model providers used were OpenAI, An-thropic, Qwen, and Moonshot AI. Qwen 3.6 was run on a local Ollama server with 2 Nvidia A6000 GPUs; all other models were accessed via API. Top performing LLMs included Anthropic’s Claude Opus 4.6 (Anthropic, 2026) ("Max" rea-soning 77.92%, "No" reasoning 77.56%), and Ope-nAI’s GPT 5.5 (OpenAI, 2026) ("High" reasoning 75.94%, "Low" reasoning 74.87%). This perfor-mance is consistent with the growing model intelli-gence over time – showing that, in general, recent models perform better than older models, larger models outperform smaller models, and higher rea-soning effort outperforms non-reasoning models. However, all these patterns have clear and notable exceptions, showing that identifying human dia-logue is more than just model intelligence. 

4 Discussion 

Of the preliminary models tested, GPTZero per-formed best, at 86-89%. This implies that contem-porary statistical detection methods can be effective at mixed multi-turn AI-detection, with appropriate scaffolding. If statistical tools like GPTZero are successful, why do we need to benchmark this ca-pability in LLMs? 

Semantic vs. Statistical Detection In Figure 1, we break down the top-performing judge mod-els thusfar by Witness models, and an interesting pattern emerges. Though GPTZero is a relatively successful detector for most LLM witnesses, its accuracy dropped to 59.02% for Eliza 1. As a token-probability-based model, GPTZero is trained to ex-tract statistical patterns from popular LLMs rather than semantic qualities of text. However, when it is presented with a chatbot that is obviously AI, but different from its training data, it fails notably. By contrast, Opus-4.6 and GPT-5.5 identified Eliza as AI 100% of the time. This suggests that GPTZero is more of an LLM detector than an AI detector. It may not be as effective with a different architecture or data distribution. 

Persona Prompt Attack Opus-4.6, GPT-5.5, and the human judge’s performance dropped when the AI witness was created with a persona prompt, as opposed to no persona (Opus: 95.80% vs. 59.04%; GPT-5.5: 93.36% vs. 57.56%; Human: 68.53% vs 39.85%), while GPTZero’s performance remains consistent. The semantic approach considers deeper parts of the text, such as coherence, pragmatics, con-versational dynamics, reasoning, and contextual consistency across turns. This approach seems to be more susceptible to prompt-engineering attacks, however, in theory, may be less susceptible to statis-tical attacks. Future studies should empirically ex-plore the differences between these two approaches, including their efficacy across domains, suscepti-bility to attacks, and whether a combined approach improves accuracy and resilience. 

Inverse Turing Test The Inverse Turing test, implemented here in the context of AI detection benchmarking, is primarily a differentiation task. The ITT was said to be passed if a machine judge performs on-par with human judges, as this would demonstrate a latent model of human vs. machine behavior that is similar and equal to that of humans (Watt, 1996). In our setting, however, several LLM judge models performed higher than human judges. What does this imply about their naive psychology? Watt theorized that higher-than-human perfor-mance could indicate dissimilar intelligence with superior discriminative ability. However, the best-performing judge models were released later than the witness models they evaluated. Superior perfor-mance may partly stem from exposure to outputs from these witness models during training. Future work should examine whether newer models are equally effective at detecting content from contem-porary systems within and across model families. 

AI Agents & Theory of Mind The Inverse test probes LLM theory of mind. Understanding and benchmarking this capability in LLMs is increas-ingly important. As AI applications become more agentic, (M)LLMs autonomously interact with hu-mans and other AI systems. In order to be effec-tive in multi-agent interaction, autonomous agents should have the ability to discriminate human and AI behavior. Recognizing LLM content will enable these agents to become active partners in protecting human spaces and, eventually, protect themselves from adversarial AI agents. 

5 Conclusion 

Human-AI differentiation in multi-turn settings is both a challenging detection task and a critical ca-pability for future LLMs. Rapid advances in model and agentic capabilities make live benchmarking essential for evaluating these systems over time. We built I NVERSE TURING BENCH , a benchmark for measuring how well models can discriminate between multi-turn human/human and human/AI dialogue. Preliminary results found that statistical and semantic approaches leave blind spots. Our ini-tial findings call for further evaluation of different tools, such as agentic tools or models that combine statistical-semantic approaches. 

6 Limitations 

This benchmark uses transcripts from interactive Turing tests, which built witness personas using 4GPT-4.5, GPT-4o, and LLaMA-405B. The bench-mark may not account for detection of more recent models, or other model families. The human judges in the original Turing test often use adversarial questioning to detect AI, so transcripts may con-tain more relevant information for detection than unsuspecting Human-AI conversations. Another important limitation of the benchmark is the lack of diversity in the AI witness persona. Risks of this study include use of the benchmark or its dataset for training LLMs to be less detectable; to adapt the benchmark for such future challenges, we would re-release with more complex dialogues. Future work could test longer conversations where neither agent is suspecting AI, add more diverse personas, or conduct interactive Inverted tests.
